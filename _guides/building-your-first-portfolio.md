## <a id="step-1"></a>Decide what money is genuinely investable

Before allocation strategy, a filter. Money you might need within about three years does not
belong in stocks. Not because stocks are bad, but because you cannot control when they are down.

A concrete failure mode: you save \$2,000 for a car, put it in a fund in September, the market
drops 20% in February, and in March you must sell at a loss because the transmission died. The
investment wasn't wrong. You just needed it to be somewhere else.

So separate the money into piles first:

| Pile | Where it goes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Next 12 months of spending | Checking account | Needs to be spendable today |
| 1-3 year goals (car, laptop, college stuff) | High-yield savings | Predictable value beats growth |
| 5+ years out | Investments | Can survive the drawdowns and recover |

An emergency buffer comes before any of this. If a surprise \$400 bill would send you to a credit
card, that buffer is the higher-return investment than any stock you could pick, because credit
card interest reliably outruns market returns.

## <a id="step-2"></a>Pick your time horizon honestly

Your horizon is how long the money can sit untouched. It matters more than which stocks you own,
and it is the input almost everyone skips.

Twenty-year horizon: a 40% drop is a footnote. You'll see it recover and then some, several times
over if history repeats.

Two-year horizon: the same drop is a genuine problem, because there isn't time to wait it out.

Most teens reading this have the *longest* horizon of anyone in the market - decades before the
money is likely needed. That's a real advantage and the main reason starting early with small
amounts beats starting late with large ones. It's worth noting that it only counts as an advantage
if you actually leave the money alone for those decades.

## <a id="step-3"></a>Start with one broad fund, not seven stocks

The highest-value decision available to a beginner is also the dullest: buy a **broad total-market
index ETF** and hold it.

Reasoning rather than assertion. A total-market fund holds essentially every significant listed US
company, so one purchase gives you diversification across industries and company sizes that would
take real capital and dozens of trades to assemble yourself. Its expense ratio - the annual fee -
is typically well under a quarter of one percent, meaning \$25/year on \$10,000. And over long
periods, most professional fund managers who try to beat such an index fail to, which is a strange
fact to sit with given their resources.

That's not a claim that picking stocks never works. Some people are good at it. It's a claim that
the skill takes years to develop, so the cheap way to get market exposure while you're developing
it is through a fund.

A reasonable starting shape:

- **70-90% total-market index ETF** - the boring engine
- **10-30% cash or short-term bonds** - dry powder and sleep
- **0-10% individual stock picks** - your learning budget

Adjust the middle number up if seeing red would make you panic-sell. Sleeping through a crash
matters more than a slightly better expected return you can't psychologically hold onto.

## <a id="step-4"></a>Size single-stock bets at 5-10% maximum

You will want to own shares in companies you actually like. Fine - and useful, since owning
something makes you pay attention to earnings reports you'd otherwise scroll past.

Just cap it. Say 5-10% of the total portfolio in any one name, and something like 20-25% across
all individual picks combined.

Here's the logic. If one position is 5% of your portfolio and goes to zero, you lose 5% and
learn an enormous lesson cheaply. If it's 60% and goes to zero, you may conclude investing is a
scam and quit entirely - which is the actually expensive outcome, because it costs you every
future year of compounding.

The difference between those two scenarios is position sizing, not forecast accuracy. You get
zero control over whether your pick was right. You get complete control over how much it can
hurt.

One more thing worth resisting: concentrating in companies you've heard of on TikTok. If a stock
is being pushed hard to teenagers, consider who benefits from teenagers buying it now.

## <a id="step-5"></a>Set up a recurring buy instead of timing entries

Automate a fixed dollar amount on a fixed date - say \$25 on the 1st - and let it buy whatever
the price is.

This is dollar-cost averaging, and its benefit is usually described wrongly. It does not reliably
improve returns versus investing the whole sum immediately (a lump sum usually wins on average,
because markets rise more often than they fall). What it does is remove the decision you're
worst at making, which is *when*.

Waiting for a better entry price is how a lot of beginners end up never investing at all: the
market always looks either too high or too scary this week. A scheduled purchase sidesteps the
argument entirely. You were going to buy anyway; you just saved yourself the month of deliberating
about it.

Also check what the automation buys. Some brokers default recurring buys to a single stock you
last traded. Confirm it's pointed at your fund.

## <a id="step-6"></a>Write down the rebalancing rule before you need it

Rebalancing means selling what's grown and buying what's shrunk, to return to your target split.

Say you want 80% fund / 20% singles. The singles do well and are now 45% of the portfolio. You are
now carrying far more single-company risk than you decided to carry - you just drifted into it.
Selling some winners and buying the fund restores the target. It's mechanically "sell high, buy
low," performed for reasons other than a feeling.

Choose the trigger while calm, because the two moments you'll want to break the rule are precisely
the moments it exists:

- **After a rally** - "why sell the winners, they keep going"
- **During a crash** - "why buy falling things, this is throwing money away"

Either set a date (once a year, your birthday works) or a drift threshold (rebalance when anything
moves more than 5 percentage points off target). Both work. Unwritten rules do not, because an
unwritten rule gets renegotiated live by whoever is currently scared.

And resist checking. Weekly balance-checking correlates with worse decisions - more trading, more
panic-selling. Monthly at most, unless something fundamental changed.
