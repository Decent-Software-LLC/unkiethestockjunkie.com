Reported profit is an estimate assembled under rules that allow judgment, prepared by people whose bonuses and share
options frequently depend on where the number lands. None of which makes it dishonest, and none of which makes it a
measurement. Earnings quality asks a narrow, answerable question: how much of this year's reported profit represents cash
the business actually keeps, versus timing decisions, estimates and items that will not recur? Every accounting scandal
you have heard of involved the second category dressed as the first, so this is the highest-yield reading skill available
to somebody who cannot visit factories or interview customers.

## The judgment zone

Accounting records revenue when earned and expenses when matched against it rather than when cash moves, which is
reasonable in principle and elastic in practice. Accruals live in the gap: goods shipped on extended terms count as sales
while payment remains outstanding, inventory built ahead of demand carries costs onto the balance sheet instead of the
income statement, warranties and expected returns are estimated years ahead, development work gets capitalised rather
than expensed depending on management's view of future benefit, subscription revenue gets recognised across periods under
policies companies may revise. One-time items sit in the same neighbourhood: proceeds from selling a building, a legal
settlement reversal, tax adjustments, restructuring charges recurring every year for six years running -- a category
beginners should assume is fictional until proven otherwise. When regulators' own enforcement history includes cases where
ordinary operating expenses were reclassified as long-term assets to make quarterly results appear dramatically better, the
principle that presentation can change the story without changing operations is not hypothetical.

The mechanism worth internalising is simple: **profit that does not arrive as cash has to be sitting somewhere**, usually
in receivables growing faster than sales or inventory growing faster than either. Watch those two lines against revenue
over several quarters and half of aggressive reporting becomes visible without any forensic training. Rising receivable
days alongside flat sales means customers are paying later or being offered terms they didn't request; combined with a big
December, that pattern has a name in enforcement literature.

## Checks you can run in ten minutes

Start with [the cash flow statement](/articles/cash-flow-statement-following-the-money/) rather than the headline. Compare
operating cash flow to net profit across five years, not one: a durable converter runs near or above reported profit
through cycles, while a firm whose profits consistently outrun cash generation is financing its own accounting. Compute
the gap per share each year and see which direction it drifts [-- free cash flow explains why the difference compounds](/articles/free-cash-flow-the-money-that-matters/).
Then read [the income statement](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/) for composition, not level: is gross
margin stable while reported growth comes from acquisitions? Did revenue rise while segment definitions quietly changed?
Are "adjusted" figures adding back the same costs each quarter, which converts ordinary expenses into optional ones? Share
count belongs in the check too, because profit per share can hold steady while total profit stagnates if issuance funds
operations -- growth in shares outstanding transfers value away silently.

Search the document for vocabulary that predicts unpleasantness: *impairment*, *restatement*, *material weakness*,
*change in accounting policy*, *related party*, *extended payment terms*, *right of return*, *deferred revenue*,
*going concern*. Each hit deserves the paragraph around it, since disclosure obligations force bad news into the text
even when press releases omit it. Cross-check the numbers against
[the audited filing's notes](/articles/annual-reports-the-ceo-s-letter-to-shareholders/) where policies are stated plainly
and prior-year comparatives expose revisions. Auditor behaviour counts as evidence: resignations, late filings, disputed
opinions and frequent partner rotation deserve understanding before forming any view on growth.

Named cautionary cases help the habit stick, provided they're used as pattern recognition rather than gossip. Revenue booked
for transactions that never generated cash; borrowings hidden inside structures nobody consolidated; expenses presented as
assets -- all three occurred at enterprises widely known at the time as exemplars of management skill, praised by analysts
and followed by institutions far better resourced than any retail investor. Their statements had been readable throughout.
What separated insiders from outsiders was not access but attention to unfashionable pages.

A specific temptation deserves naming because it recurs in teen-facing finance content: unusually profitable-looking
quarters driven by items unrelated to operations. A company reporting about \$1.58 billion in revenue from regulatory
credits in one year -- a figure its own filings disclosed -- illustrated the general problem beautifully: credit sales
depended on other manufacturers' shortfalls, arrived in lumpy amounts, and had nothing to do with vehicle margins, yet sat
inside the number everyone quoted. Ask of every strong quarter whether the driver repeated last year, will repeat next, and
belongs to the core operation. Anything failing two of three tests should be excluded from your view of what the business
earns -- the exercise sometimes removes most of a celebrated quarter's excitement, which is information in itself.

Earnings quality isn't cynicism; healthy businesses survive this reading easily, often improving confidence enough to act
while sceptics deliberate. But the discipline matters more than any ratio learned so far, because every valuation you build
stands on the profit figure, and garbage multiplied by generous assumptions still produces garbage. When the checks fail
repeatedly, the conclusion is not that you need deeper modelling -- it is that you lack the ability to verify something
consequential, and holding unverified things with money you need is gambling wearing a spreadsheet.

This is educational explanation of financial-reporting judgment, not financial advice or a recommendation to buy or sell
anything; no named company is endorsed or accused here, illustrative figures come from public filings and expire, all
readings carry interpretation risk, and investing involves losing money even after careful analysis.
