If you’d put $100 into the S&P 500 the month you were born and never touched it, you’d probably have more than your car by now.

That’s not magic. It’s compound growth. But here’s the catch: the company’s earnings don’t always tell the whole story. A stock might look like it’s crushing it because of a one-time sale or a weird accounting trick. That’s where "earnings quality" comes in.

Think of it like checking a used sneaker’s resale app. You see the price is $200, but the soles are falling apart and the box is torn. Would you pay full price? Probably not. Earnings quality is the inspection report for a company’s profits. It tells you if the money they’re reporting is real or just smoke and mirrors.

Accruals are the accounting trick that makes earnings look better than they are. Imagine your favorite game’s ranked tiers. You grind for weeks to hit Platinum, but the game counts your wins differently than your losses. Accruals do that with revenue. For the mechanics of where each line sits, see [Income Statement: Revenue, Expenses, Profits](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/). Instead of counting cash when it hits the bank, they count it when it’s earned. Like if a company sells $100 of sneakers on credit, they count the $100 as revenue immediately, even though the customer hasn’t paid yet. It’s like getting points in a game before you’ve actually scored.

But accruals aren’t the only problem. One-time gains or losses can make earnings look wild. Take Tesla in 2020. It reported about $1.58 billion in revenue from regulatory credits, the allowances it sells to other automakers to help them meet emissions rules. Its total net income that year came in near $721 million, which means those credits accounted for more than all of its reported profit. That’s like your friend posting a great month because they sold their bike, not because the paycheck got bigger. The headline looked strong; the recurring business underneath was doing far less of the work. Whether you think that matters depends on whether credit sales keep happening at that rate, which is exactly the question an accrual-minded reader asks.

This is why forensic accounting matters. It’s not about catching fraud—it’s about seeing through the noise. Like checking a creator’s upload history on YouTube. If their "viral" video got 10 million views but they deleted 500,000 for spam, the real reach is way lower. Same with earnings: if a company’s profits jump because they sold a building or wrote off debt, that’s not sustainable.

Here’s what goes wrong: if you trust the headline number, you might buy a stock that looks great but is actually bleeding cash. Like buying a sneaker that’s marked down from $200 to $150 because the store’s desperate to clear inventory. The price looks good, but the value’s shaky.

So what can you do today? Pull up a real filing rather than a summary. [Annual Reports: The CEO’s Letter to Shareholders](/articles/annual-reports-the-ceo-s-letter-to-shareholders/) covers reading one without falling asleep, and the specific habit to start with is comparing reported profit to cash from operations side by side, which is the subject of [Free Cash Flow: The Money That Matters](/articles/free-cash-flow-the-money-that-matters/). Look for phrases like "one-time charge" or "non-recurring." Compare two funds’ expense ratios—like checking the fee for a data plan. If one charges 0.5% and another 0.2%, that’s $30 less per $10,000 invested over a year. Small numbers add up.

Earnings quality isn’t about being a finance nerd. It’s about seeing the real story behind the headline. Because if you’re investing with money you’ve saved from mowing lawns or working at Starbucks, you deserve to know what you’re really buying.

This is education, not financial advice. Investing involves risk, and nothing here guarantees you’ll make money. But understanding the fine print? That’s a tool worth having.
