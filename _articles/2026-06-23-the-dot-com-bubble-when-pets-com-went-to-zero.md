In early 2000 you could not open a newspaper without a story about someone whose cousin quit college to run a website delivering groceries, pet food or dog treats, valued as though profitability were a phase companies grow out of. Then prices collapsed so thoroughly that entire categories of household-name businesses vanished, and anyone who bought at the peak waited more than a decade just to get back to even.

## The mechanism, minus the drama

A bubble is not a rumor or a scandal. It is what happens when the price of an asset gets high enough that the only remaining buyers are people hoping to sell it higher still, and everyone can see this, and everyone expects to exit before it matters. The internet genuinely was transformative, which is what made the pricing defensible for years: being early to something real does not automatically mean any purchase price is rational, and those two facts got fused together in the popular retelling.

Valuations detached from cash flow across the sector while ordinary metrics kept working fine elsewhere. Established profitable firms traded at modest multiples; unprofitable ones with memorable web addresses commanded billions. No accounting change caused this; thousands of individual people each reasoned that the previous quarter's gains would continue into the next one.

The index most associated with the episode tells the cleanest version of the story. The Nasdaq Composite topped out in March 2000 at a little over five thousand points and did not stop falling until late 2002, ending roughly three-quarters below its peak. That single figure explains why "but the technology works" is not itself an investment thesis: broadband adoption continued exactly as bulls predicted throughout the collapse, and prices still fell apart, because adoption timing and entry price are separate questions. [Price-to-Earnings Ratio: The Most Popular Metric](/articles/price-to-earnings-ratio-the-most-popular-metric/) covers the kind of boring arithmetic nobody consulted mid-boom.

## What survivors actually looked like

Pets.com raised money on a beloved mascot, spent heavily on television advertising including a prime-time spot during 1999's biggest broadcast, delivered bulky cheap goods at negative margins nationwide, and shut down inside a year of listing. Shareholders recovered nothing. eToys followed almost the identical path through the 1999 holiday season, having risen steeply from its offering price before bankruptcy eliminated the equity entirely; Christmas demand turned out to be shippable profitably by warehouse incumbents who already owned the logistics. Amazon survived but lost the overwhelming majority of its market value along the way, which surprises people who remember only where it ended up, and Cisco briefly became among the world's most valuable companies selling routers for networks everybody assumed were finished being built.

Notice the asymmetry. Investors who had bought office-supply or brokerage incumbents at similar valuations rode those same internet developments to eventual profits, because those businesses already earned money and could add channels instead of inventing them from scratch. Technology being real never validated paying arbitrary amounts for it. ## Why this belongs on your reading list specifically

Every generation gets its version with its own plausible technology, its own stories about friends who got rich, and its own dismissal of anybody asking about profits, so pattern recognition transfers better than predictions do. When hype arrives, several things happen simultaneously: dissent gets mocked as failing to understand the future, valuations become off-limits, and people near the top describe caution as missing out. If that sounds familiar from anything currently circulating about tokens, artificial intelligence names, or a stock promoted in comment sections, the useful response is not skepticism toward technology but toward *price*: asking what earnings support this number and whether they need to materialize impossibly fast to justify it. See [Reading Financial News: Separating Signal from Noise](/articles/reading-financial-news-separating-signal-from-noise/) for the same discipline applied to daily coverage, and [Investing Myths Debunked](/articles/investing-myths-debunked/) for beliefs that survive crashes unchanged.

## Try this today, fifteen minutes

Find one company everyone currently considers inevitable, then locate its trailing twelve months of reported profit, free cash flow, and the multiple between those figures, all available on its investor-relations pages rather than social posts.

Write one sentence completing this prompt: the specific way this business earns money its competitors cannot easily copy is ___. Now write a second sentence: if growth slowed to half what enthusiasts assume, my purchase price still makes sense because ___. Failing either blank doesn't make the stock bad; it means you'd be buying participation in enthusiasm rather than ownership of documented economics, which is a choice worth making consciously, since that's exactly the position people occupied in March 2000 while everything felt obvious.
