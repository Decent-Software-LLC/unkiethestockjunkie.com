A chart of a stock you own can make you feel brilliant or terrified in the same minute without a single new fact arriving. That's not a flaw in charts, it's what happens when you read pictures as predictions. Line charts show one thing reliably: what happened. Everything else people claim to extract from them deserves scrutiny before you act on it.

## The minimum vocabulary

**Time axis**, horizontal. Zoomed out for decades, zoomed into minutes intraday, and identical-looking shapes mean entirely different things at different scales, which is the first trap; there's no meaningful "reversal pattern" that survives switching from hourly to weekly bars.

**Price axis**, vertical. Usually linear, sometimes **logarithmic**. Switch between them and long-running winners look completely different, because logarithmic scaling measures percentage change rather than dollar change. A stock going 100 to 200 occupies the same height as 200 to 400 on log scale, but double the vertical space on linear. Ask which one you're looking at before trusting your eyes, since linear exaggerates recent swings in high-priced names.

**Candlesticks** compress four values per period: open, close, high, low. Bodies show open-to-close movement, wicks show extremes reached then abandoned. Long lower wick means buyers absorbed selling near the bottom. It describes who was willing to transact where during that window, nothing more. See [What Is Volume and Why Does It Matter?](/articles/what-is-volume-and-why-does-it-matter/) before treating any formation as confirmation of anything.

## What lines actually represent

**Moving averages** smooth noise by averaging prior closes over some window. Crossing above or below one is a *rule*, not a signal with inherent validity; length choice is arbitrary and easily tuned until it "predicts," which is data-mining dressed up visually. Any sufficiently flexible indicator eventually matches price history perfectly and still tells you zero about tomorrow.

**Support and resistance** are levels where price reversed previously. The mechanism isn't mystical, resting orders and remembered prices attract participants at round numbers like fifty dollars because enough people choose them, creating self-reinforcing effects that fade once widely noticed. Useful for placing limit orders sensibly. Not evidence of anything fundamental.

For serious treatment including its limits see [Technical Analysis: Reading the Charts](/articles/technical-analysis-reading-the-charts/). Short version: academic evidence on technical trading is weak at best; large institutional traders use technical vocabulary for execution timing while building positions from fundamentals or quantitative factors. When actual practitioners treat these tools casually, adjust your confidence accordingly.

## Two biases charts weaponize

**Narrative after the fact.** Every peak has an explanation once visible and none during. Draw three trendlines on the same messy chart and find three people each defending theirs passionately. If two experts disagree using identical pixels, the chart wasn't producing the conclusion.

**Anchoring.** You paid $48. Price drops to $30 and you hold waiting to break even, mentally fixed on a number that holds no authority over the company whatsoever. Your entry cost is invisible to markets and irrelevant to future outcomes, yet charts constantly remind you of it, generating losses sustained purely for psychological comfort. That instinct gets quantified in [Loss Aversion: Why Losses Feel Worse Than Gains](/articles/loss-aversion-why-losses-feel-worse-than-gains/); reading it next time you stare at a red position matters far more than learning another candlestick name.

## Try this today, twenty minutes

Open free charting on any platform. Pull up an index fund you could plausibly own. Set it to maximum range, usually two decades back, on logarithmic scale. Mark two moments: a year of deep pessimism that felt permanent, and one year later. Notice how nearly all recovered, and confirm nobody inside those valleys knew that.

Then switch to linear and watch the identical data produce a fundamentally different emotional impression. Experiencing that shift does more to inoculate against chart-driven panic than memorizing twenty pattern definitions.

Finally check whether anyone sold in the middle of that valley and stayed out through recovery, common in documented flows during panics, giving you the base rate for what acting on chart fear costs over decades. Then apply the same habit to fundamentals using [Income Statement: Revenue, Expenses, Profits](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/), which won't tell you perfect entry either but keeps you anchored in something real rather than shape recognition.
