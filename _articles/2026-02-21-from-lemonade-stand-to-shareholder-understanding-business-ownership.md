You own a share of Apple. Congratula— what did you actually get? Not a say in product design, not a slice of the Apple Park café, not so much as a voting invite to anything. Understanding what a share is and isn't explains most of the weird behavior on finance social media.

## Ownership, minus control

A stock certificate is a claim on part of a company's assets and earnings after everyone else gets paid. It's real ownership, but it's *residual* ownership: suppliers, employees, lenders, and the tax man all stand ahead of you. If things go badly, shareholders are last in line, which means they're first to get nothing.

You get three things: a proportionate claim on whatever profit gets distributed, roughly one vote per share on electing the board of directors, and the right to sell. You do not get to tell anyone what to build. The gap between owning something and controlling it is the entire structure of public markets.

## The three legal shapes

**Sole proprietorship**, one person, everything. You keep profits, you take every loss personally, and your personal belongings are exposed if the business gets sued. Every kid with a lawn-mowing Instagram page is one of these whether they filed anything or not.

**Partnership**, two or more people doing the same thing, sharing profits and decisions. Cheaper to set up than a corporation and just as dangerous, since you can be liable for a partner's mistakes. If you and two friends resell sneakers together and one of you gets taken by a buyer dispute, that can land on all of you depending on structure.

**Corporation**, a legal entity separate from its owners. It can sign contracts, own property, and get sued in its own name, and owners generally lose only what they invested. That liability wall is why large businesses incorporate, and it's also the tradeoff: corporations file separately, pay their own taxes, disclose finances publicly, and answer to a board.

When a private corporation decides to sell shares to anyone on an exchange, it "goes public" and becomes a **publicly traded** company. That listing is the moment your $50 becomes possible at all; see [What Is an IPO? When Companies Go Public](/articles/what-is-an-ipo-when-companies-go-public/).

## Who actually controls the giants

Apple is owned, in fragments, by hundreds of thousands of holders. Its largest positions aren't people, they're index fund managers like Vanguard and BlackRock, holding those shares *on behalf of* millions of ordinary investors including plenty of teenagers with custodial accounts. Nobody there picks your stocks; they're obliged to buy whatever's in the index, which is how passive money ended up as the biggest owner of corporate America.

Nike has operated with concentrated voting control tied to founder Phil Knight and family holdings, meaning economic ownership and actual control sat in different places. Meta follows a similar pattern with Zuckerberg holding super-voting shares that give him far more influence than his percentage stake alone implies. That kind of dual-class setup means you can own a share, cast your vote, and have it matter almost nothing, permanently.

None of this makes those investments bad. It does mean "I'm a partial owner" carries less practical authority than it sounds like over a group chat, and it's worth knowing before repeating someone else's thesis about what the company "should" be doing.

## Do this today, ten minutes

Open any investor-relations page. Search "<company> investor relations" and find its proxy statement, usually under Governance or SEC filings. You'll see exactly what shareholders vote on each year: director elections, auditor approval, executive pay ("say on pay"), occasional proposals from activist shareholders.

Skim the ballot. Notice how many items are essentially rubber-stamps, then check how much stock management actually owns. When insiders hold little and voting control is locked elsewhere, your interests and theirs may not align, and now you know where to look instead of guessing. For what the numbers in those filings mean, start with [Annual Reports: The CEO's Letter to Shareholders](/articles/annual-reports-the-ceo-s-letter-to-shareholders/) and work backward to [Income Statement: Revenue, Expenses, Profits](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/).
