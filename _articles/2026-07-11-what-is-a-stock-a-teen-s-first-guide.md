You can buy a piece of Nike for less than a pair of socks. That fact alone should make you suspicious, because if something this cheap were a reliable way to build money everyone would do it and it wouldn't work anymore. It works *usually*, over long stretches, badly in bursts. Knowing which is which is the whole subject.

## What a share is

A stock is a claim on part of a company. Buy one share and you own a fraction of the business, its products, brand, employee contracts, factories, everything, shared with every other holder. This is different from lending money to a company, which is what bonds are: lenders get fixed payments and priority if things collapse, owners get whatever's left after everyone else gets paid.

That residual position explains most stock behavior. When a company does well, price rises because that leftover amount is expected to grow. When it struggles, shareholders absorb the loss first, sometimes entirely. Nobody has ever been legally required to keep your investment intact.

## Why prices move when nothing changed

Prices reflect what buyers will pay now, not a formula computed from last quarter's profit. So they move on expectations, and expectations shift on interest rates, competitor announcements, an analyst note, a viral post about some app nobody had heard of Monday. A company can report identical results two quarters running and see its shares swing 30% between them purely because investors re-priced what the *next* five years look like.

Long term, price converges toward actual business performance. Short term, it tracks mood. Confusing those timeframes causes nearly all beginner losses; see [Bulls vs Bears: Understanding Market Trends](/articles/bulls-vs-bears-understanding-market-trends/).

## Two ways a stock pays you

**Appreciation**: sell later for more than you paid. Nothing forces this and taxes apply when you realize gains.

**Dividends**: cash distributed to owners periodically, decided by the board, cuttable without warning. Many large profitable companies pay none at all, permanently, spending instead on growth. The mechanics are in [What Is a Dividend? Getting Paid to Own Stocks](/articles/what-is-a-dividend-getting-paid-to-own-stocks/) and worth reading before treating yield as income.

## How ownership actually works in practice

You don't buy directly from Apple's office. Orders route through a broker to an exchange, which matches buyers against sellers. You'll never appear on any list of Apple owners in a way management notices; your shares usually sit in street name under the broker's custody arrangements, and fractional shares aren't technically registered shares at all. This doesn't weaken your economic claim, but it means paperwork questions go to your custodian rather than the SEC hotline. See [What Is a Broker and How to Choose One?](/articles/what-is-a-broker-and-how-to-choose-one/) before opening anything, since fees and account types differ meaningfully even when both advertise zero commission.

As a minor you cannot open a brokerage account alone. Adult-sponsored options include a **custodial account**, where funds are legally yours at eighteen or twenty-one depending on state, and a **Custodial Roth IRA**, available only if you have earned income from actual work. Those routes, including their real traps around financial aid and control transferring at majority, are laid out step-by-step in [Opening Your First Brokerage Account](/guides/opening-your-first-brokerage-account/). Don't skip it. Custodial accounts quietly complicate college funding and a surprising number of families discover that late.

## Try this today, twenty minutes

Pick one company whose product you used today, phone, game, drink, clothing. Find its investor-relations page and read the most recent press release header, then locate three numbers: revenue, net income, and shares outstanding.

Divide net income by shares outstanding. That's earnings per share, and it's a genuine measure of profit attributable to each unit you could own. Now find its current share price and divide by that EPS figure. You've just computed a P/E ratio, the single most-used valuation shortcut, from primary sources rather than someone's summary.

Don't yet judge whether the result is good. Just notice how much easier the question becomes once you know what to look for, which is more than most people who trade achieve, and revisit [Understanding the Stock Market: A Playground for Investors](/articles/understanding-the-stock-market-a-playground-for-investors/) for the vocabulary you'll need next.
