If you'd put $100 into the S&P 500 the month you were born and never touched it, you'd probably have more than your car by now.

Short squeezes happen when a stock gets hated by everyone, then suddenly gets loved by a bunch of people at once. Think of it like a crowded hallway where everyone’s trying to get out, but one person suddenly starts running the other way. The crowd surges backward, and the person running into them gets pushed hard. In stocks, the "crowd" are short sellers betting the price will fall. The "person running the other way" is the group of buyers who suddenly pile in, forcing short sellers to buy back shares at higher prices to cover their bets.

GameStop’s stock ticker is GME. In early 2021, it traded around $20. Hedge funds had shorted it heavily, meaning they bet it would drop to $5 or $10. Then Reddit users on r/wallstreetbets started buying GME en masse. The price jumped to $400 in weeks. Short sellers who’d borrowed shares at $20 now had to buy them back at $400. That squeeze cost them billions.

Meme stocks are shares of companies that get popular online, often because of jokes or viral trends. They’re not chosen for strong businesses but for their potential to move fast. Tesla, AMC, and Bed Bath & Beyond all became meme stocks at different times. Meme stocks move because of social media hype, not earnings reports.

Short sellers borrow shares from brokers, sell them immediately, and hope to buy them back cheaper later. If the price rises, they lose money. GameStop’s rise in 2021 was extreme: short interest hit 140% of the float. That meant more shares were sold short than actually existed. When buyers pushed the price up, short sellers had to scramble to cover, driving the price even higher.

The danger is that meme stocks can crash just as fast as they rise. If the hype fades and no real buyers show up, the price can plummet. In 2021, GameStop fell from $400 to $100 in months. Retail investors who bought at the top got burned. Short squeezes aren’t sustainable; they’re fueled by emotion, not fundamentals.

You can’t short squeeze without real buyers. Meme stocks often lack strong financials, so their prices rely on speculation. If the crowd loses interest, the stock can drop hard. This isn’t investing—it’s gambling with other people’s money.

Try this today: Go to Yahoo Finance, search for GME, and look at the "short interest" percentage. Compare it to a stable stock like Coca-Cola (KO). If short interest is over 20%, it’s at risk for a squeeze. But don’t act on it—just observe.

This is education, not financial advice. Investing involves risk. You can lose money.
