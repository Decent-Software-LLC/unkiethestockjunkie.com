Two stocks are open in your app. One trades at 45 times earnings and pays nothing. The other trades at 11 times, has paid a dividend since before your parents were born, and nobody talks about it at lunch.

One of those is called growth, the other value. And the labels describe what you're *paying for*, not which is better.

## Growth: you're buying the curve, not the company today

A growth stock is priced on what it might become. Revenue compounds fast, and nearly every dollar goes back into the business, new products, new markets, hiring. Profits are thin or absent on purpose; spending now is the strategy.

Amazon is the clean example. In the early 2000s it was a textbook growth stock: relentless reinvestment into its e-commerce platform, minimal reported profit, and a valuation that made no sense on any given quarter's earnings. People who bought it then and held through years of "it'll never make money" takes did extremely well. Plenty who bought it at a slightly different moment did not, which is the part the success story hides.

That's the risk in one line. A growth price already assumes the future arrives. When it slips, there's no earnings cushion underneath, so the multiple collapses and the stock can drop 60% while revenue is still rising.

## Value: you're buying $1.00 for seventy cents, allegedly

A value stock is an established business trading below what its numbers suggest it's worth. Steady cash flow, often a dividend, usually boring, frequently in an industry nobody wants to be caught owning.

Johnson & Johnson shows how the setup works. In 2018 its share price fell on concerns about one of its products. Investors willing to look past the headline, reasoning that a decades-long franchise doesn't evaporate over a single lawsuit cycle, were effectively pricing the company as damaged permanently when the evidence was messier than that. Whether you'd have made money depends on details like which lawsuit, which year, and what you paid, but the *reasoning* is the template: price dropped because of fear, not because the cash flows vanished.

Sometimes the fear is correct. That's the entire difficulty of value investing, and it's why [Benjamin Graham: The Father of Value Investing](/articles/benjamin-graham-the-father-of-value-investing/) spends its time on measuring cheapness rather than celebrating it. A stock can be cheap because it's misunderstood, or cheap because it's a dying business on its way to zero, and they look identical on a screenshot.

## Side by side, honestly

| | Growth | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Priced on | Future potential | Current assets and cash flow |
| Typical P/E | High | Low |
| Dividend | Rarely | Often |
| Reinvestment | Almost all profits | Partial, rest paid out |
| Downside when things slip | Violent, no cushion | Muted, but drags for years |
| Fails when | Growth stalls or was assumed wrong | Cheapness was deserved |

The P/E column is where this stops being vibes. See [Price-to-Earnings Ratio: The Most Popular Metric](/articles/price-to-earnings-ratio-the-most-popular-metric/) for how to read one without fooling yourself, because "high P/E" alone tells you nothing until you know what's inside the earnings.

## Two traps both camps fall into

**Calling anything expensive "growth."** Tesla, Nvidia and a dozen hype names get labeled growth as though that settles it. Growth isn't a compliment about volatility. It's a claim about reinvestment and unit economics, and if the growth never shows up you just own something pricey.

**Calling anything cheap "value."** A company whose revenue has fallen eight years running is not overlooked. It's declining. Value requires a plausible reason the market is wrong, and "the chart is low" is not that reason.

## Do this today, free, ten minutes

Open a screener on Webull, Fidelity or Finviz. Filter two ways:

1. Revenue growth above 20% per year, dividend yield under 1%.
2. P/E below 15, dividend yield above 2%, five consecutive profitable years.

Look at the two lists side by side. One is full of names you've heard of constantly, the other full of names you've never heard. Notice that neither list looks obviously right. That discomfort is the honest starting point, and it beats picking a style because it sounds clever.

If choosing between them feels like guessing, that's information. Most people are better off owning both across the market instead of deciding in advance, which is what [ETFs: Instant Diversification Made Easy](/articles/etfs-instant-diversification-made-easy/) exists for.

*This is education, not financial advice. No stock here is recommended, and nothing in either category has ever come with a guarantee. Both styles have periods where they underperform for years, and money put into either one can be lost.*
