Someone with 300k followers just turned $100 into a car in ninety days, and the link in their bio costs $49.99. Almost everything you'll hear about investing from people whose income depends on your attention is some version of that sentence. Here's what holds up.

## "You need money to start"

False, and it's the myth costing you the most, because waiting is the expensive choice. Fractional shares mean small recurring amounts work; plenty of brokerages let you buy $5 of an index fund, and several custodial account types exist specifically for minors with a parent as co-signer. What actually gates you isn't balance size, it's starting late. See [What Is a Broker and How to Choose One](/articles/what-is-a-broker-and-how-to-choose-one/) for what to look for when you're ready, and note that minimums are near zero now where they used to be real barriers.

The math doesn't care where you started, only how long it ran. Someone putting away lunch money at sixteen can beat someone starting at thirty with far larger deposits purely on time.

## "Investing is too complicated"

For most people, the genuinely optimal action is boring: buy a diversified low-cost index fund repeatedly, don't touch it. That requires understanding maybe four concepts, not a finance degree. Complication sells courses because complexity makes experts necessary. It's also true that picking individual stocks *is* hard, which is precisely why almost nobody should start there.

## "It's basically safe if you pick good companies"

No. Good companies go to zero occasionally, get acquired cheaply, suffer permanent industry disruption, or simply get priced for perfection and then unwind. Blue chips have lost most of their value for years on end, sometimes permanently, and even index funds have had decades-long real returns after inflation that would disappoint anyone counting on them. Anything marketed as safe-with-upside is being sold to you by someone earning a fee. Diversification reduces specific risk but never eliminates market risk.

## "You should wait until you understand it"

A polite way of saying never. You learn by holding something small enough that a bad outcome teaches rather than destroys, which is exactly what paper trading and tiny first positions are for. Try [Paper Trading: Practice Without Risk](/articles/paper-trading-practice-without-risk/) first, though know it measures discipline rather than forecasting talent since fake dollars don't trigger real fear.

## The myths specific to your feed

**"Day trading beats holding."** Retail day traders underperform buy-and-hold as a group, consistently, and most quit within a couple of years. There's survivorship in every screenshot: the winners post, the losers delete. Anyone charging for signals has chosen the reliable business model over the strategy they're advertising. If it worked as claimed, selling it would be the less profitable option.

**"Copy wealthy investors' portfolios."** Regulatory filings show what professionals bought *after* quarter-end deadlines, so by publication you're reading weeks-stale entries at prices already moved. You also can't see hedges, cash reserves, tax situations, or derivatives offsetting the position. Copying a disclosed long without knowing what it offsets is guessing dressed as research. [FOMO in Investing: Fear of Missing Out](/articles/fomo-in-investing-fear-of-missing-out/) covers why this feels so reasonable in the moment.

**"Crypto is just early-stock investing."** Sometimes literally framed as buying Amazon in 1997. Assets that pay no dividends, produce no earnings, and can't be valued by any cash-flow method price entirely off what a later buyer pays, which is a different mechanism requiring different sizing rules, not a younger version of equity ownership. Compare [Cryptocurrency vs Stocks: Key Differences](/articles/cryptocurrency-vs-stocks-key-differences/) before deciding any of it belongs in a beginner portfolio.

**"Experts agree on next quarter."** They demonstrably don't, and the confident ones in hindsight are whichever pundits happened to be legible afterward. Predictions are free to make and rarely graded publicly.

## One that's closer to true

"You need to pay for advice." A genuinely flat-fee fiduciary planner exists and can be worth it for taxes or family complexity, but most retail financial services monetize products, and paid subscriptions touting stock picks routinely underperform nothing at all. Verify registered status through public regulator databases rather than trusting a website badge, and ask one question: does this person earn more if I do more? If yes, discount everything else they say.

## Do this today, five minutes

Open the last three finance accounts you saw advertise something. For each: did they name a specific guaranteed return, use urgency language, show a lifestyle instead of a track record, or require a subscription to reveal the actual idea? Count how many triggered at least two. Now find one genuinely free primary source, like a company's own investor-relations page, and read its latest press release. Notice it never promises anything.

That contrast, breathless certainty versus dry non-commitment, is the most transferable pattern recognition in personal finance. Nearly every costly decision beginners make came from the first kind.
