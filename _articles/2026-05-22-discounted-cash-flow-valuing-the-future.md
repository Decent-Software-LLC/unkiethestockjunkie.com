A business is worth the cash it will produce between now and whenever it stops producing cash, translated into today's
money. That sentence is the entire method -- every spreadsheet you've seen pretending otherwise is decoration. Translating
future money backwards is necessary because a dollar arriving in four years cannot be spent, invested or lent today, and
arrives carrying the chance the enterprise won't survive that long. Discounting is simply the exchange rate between those
two states, set by whatever return you'd demand for putting up with waiting and risk.

Every valuation of this kind has exactly three dials. How long you forecast explicitly. What happens after you stop
forecasting. And the rate you discount at. Change any one and the answer moves substantially, which means the output is
better described as a summary of your assumptions than as a discovery about the company -- an important distinction almost
nobody states before selling you the result.

## Arithmetic you can check by hand

Take a made-up business throwing off \$100,000 a year of genuine cash for ten years and demanding a ten percent return.
Discount each year separately -- the tenth year's payment counts for roughly thirty-eight cents on the dollar today -- and
the stack lands near six hundred thousand dollars. Shift the required return to twelve percent and the same cash flows come
to roughly five-sixths of that. Nothing about the business changed; a subjective dial moved, and buyers who treat the first
figure as "the value" have actually just expressed a mood in currency format.

Now consider what happens when the explicit forecast ends. Businesses outlive most spreadsheets, so analysts append a
**terminal value**, typically assuming cash grows forever at some modest rate. With a two percent perpetual rate and a ten
percent discount requirement, the final forecast year gets multiplied by something like twelve or thirteen to produce the
residual value -- frequently the majority of the total computation. Widen either dial slightly and watch what occurs: the
same nine-hundred-dollar figure becomes eleven hundred or seven-fifty depending entirely on assumptions about a future
decade nobody can observe. This is not a flaw in the arithmetic; it is arithmetic faithfully reporting how little you know.
[Compounding](/articles/compound-interest-the-eighth-wonder-of-the-world/) works symmetrically in both directions once the
horizon extends.

Two habits convert this from fiction into analysis. First, work in **ranges with labels**: a pessimistic case built on
current margins eroding, a middle case matching recent history, an optimistic case requiring several favourable things at
once -- and refuse to average them into false precision, since a single blended number conceals the disagreement inside
itself. Second, invert the exercise. Rather than asking what a company is worth, ask what the current price implies: what
growth rate, held for how many years, at what discount, would justify what buyers are paying right now? Almost always the
market is pricing something more confident than reality supports, or more pessimistic than evidence warrants, and discovering
which is far more informative than admiring your own forecast. Reversing the question also exposes how much of a headline
multiple rests on the last decade having been unusual [-- decomposing returns shows what a high rating assumes](/articles/price-to-earnings-ratio-the-most-popular-metric/).

## Where models quietly lie

**Using profit as a proxy for cash.** Profits include non-cash estimates and exclude reinvestment requirements. The input
must be cash the owners could actually extract after maintenance spending -- [the difference is where disappointment lives](/articles/free-cash-flow-the-money-that-matters/).
Popular shortcuts like earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation get quoted as though they were cash
generation, which conveniently excludes the capital expenditure keeping the lights on; a capital-heavy business can look
spectacular under that convention while distributing nothing to anyone.

**Ignoring the funding of growth.** Growing revenue usually consumes working capital and equipment. Model those costs or
your forecast describes a business that expands for free. Dilution belongs here too: cash per existing share shrinks when
new shares are issued [-- per-share mechanics matter more than headline totals](/articles/earnings-growth-the-engine-of-stock-prices/).

**Perpetual growth above the economy.** Assuming decades of expansion faster than overall nominal growth is arithmetically
available in a spreadsheet and unavailable in reality; entrants, competition and customer saturation arrive eventually. Any
model whose long-run rate exceeds plausible economy-wide growth deserves scepticism regardless of how carefully its footnotes
are written.

**The discount rate as a styling choice.** Lowering it flatters everything, and there is enormous temptation to lower it for
companies you like. Fix your required return before looking at the candidate, ideally by reference to alternatives you'd
happily hold instead, and never tune it until the answer feels comfortable.

Where the method genuinely earns its place: businesses with stable, predictable cash generation and modest reinvestment
needs -- regulated operations, mature consumer franchises, anything whose economics barely change year to year. Where it
becomes theatre: early-stage ventures, deeply cyclical producers whose normalised earnings differ wildly from the current
year, and financial firms whose leverage makes equity cash flows nearly impossible to model honestly. For those categories
comparison against peers plus balance-sheet strength does more reliable work, which
[the intrinsic value walkthrough covers alongside this](/articles/fundamental-analysis-finding-intrinsic-value/).

For someone sixteen building their first spreadsheet, the deliverable isn't the number -- it's forcing yourself to write
the assumptions down where they can be contradicted. A forecast you can articulate in one paragraph beats vague confidence
every time, and the willingness to say "this estimate ranges from X to Y depending on three things I can monitor" is the
behaviour separating people who survive markets from those who discover their convictions were
[the output of a discount rate they'd unconsciously chosen](/articles/margin-of-safety-the-concept-that-protects-investors/).

This is educational explanation of a valuation technique, not financial advice or a recommendation to buy or sell anything;
all figures above are invented round numbers included so the arithmetic can be checked independently, no company is
described, small changes to assumptions swing conclusions dramatically, and investing involves risk: disciplined modeling
still routinely produces estimates that turn out wrong, and a model is never a guarantee.
