A headline says a company's stock soared after "strong earnings." The article body, four paragraphs down, mentions the beat came entirely from one-off gains and the company cut its forecast. Half the people who read the headline bought something they wouldn't have chosen with the full sentence. Headlines are written to be clicked; the information is buried where only careful readers look.

## What most of it isn't

Financial media needs volume and urgency to survive, and markets are open 6,500 minutes a week. Actual news worth acting on arrives maybe a few times a month per company. Everything else is commentary pretending to be reporting, price movement restated as explanation, or someone's opinion wearing a news layout.

Most market coverage is *reaction* rather than information: what prices did today, what someone thinks that means. This creates circular logic you'll notice once spotted. A stock falls because of a report; the report is newsworthy because the stock fell. Neither contains an underlying fact independent of the other. Once you see the loop you can't unsee it, and it dissolves about two-thirds of your feed.

A second structural issue: reporters must quote someone every day whether anything happened or not, so analysts get interviewed daily and their remarks become events. Predictions are free to make and almost never graded publicly. Track one pundit's calls against outcomes for a year if you want a permanent cure for taking confident opinions seriously. [Why Time in the Market Beats Timing the Market](/articles/why-time-in-the-market-beats-timing-the-market/) covers the cost of trading on this stuff.

## What actually counts as news

Primary sources. A company's own SEC filings (10-K annual, 10-Q quarterly, 8-K for material events), all free on the regulator's EDGAR site. Earnings press releases from investor-relations pages, not summarized by someone with an audience to keep. Prospectuses, proxy statements, and shareholder letters. None require a subscription, and none need a journalist between you and the document, which matters since summaries optimize for interest rather than accuracy.

Secondarily: official statistics releases like employment reports and inflation data, which move whole markets mechanically because algorithms react in milliseconds. Central-bank rate decisions and meeting statements. You generally don't need to interpret these well, you mainly need to know they're coming so you don't panic-trade during a volatile hour. [Stock Market Hours: When Can You Trade?](/articles/stock-market-hours-when-can-you-trade/) explains how scheduled releases distort normal sessions.

Skip: analyst target changes (mostly noise, rarely right, often self-serving since banks want underwriting business), routine upgrades/downgrades, any piece starting "here's what it means for investors," prediction articles, and every listicle built around a famous person's portfolio. See [Investing Myths Debunked](/articles/investing-myths-debunked/) for why copying disclosed holdings fails.

## The tells that something's manipulative

Adjectives doing argument's work: **soared, plunged, slammed, blows away, mauls**. These describe volatility, not information, and their job is making you feel like missing out would hurt.

Vague attribution: "analysts say," "experts believe," "reports suggest" without naming anyone checkable. If nobody's accountable, weight it near zero.

Round numbers with no source attached, and an adoption share quoted to nobody in particular. Verify figures against the filing itself before repeating them anywhere. For the vocabulary to read those documents, start at [Income Statement: Revenue, Expenses, Profits](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/) and work up to [Annual Reports: The CEO's Letter to Shareholders](/articles/annual-reports-the-ceo-s-letter-to-shareholders/).

Novelty framing around something that happens every quarter. Every three months some company "shocks" Wall Street, though beating lowered expectations is the ordinary case, not a surprise.

And always ask who benefits from your reading this right now. Answer usually: whoever gets your attention, or whoever wants you to buy or sell so liquidity exists for their side.

## The asymmetry that ruins beginners

Bad news feels urgent and good news feels like it can wait, so you sell fast on fear and delay buying on optimism. Both directions are backwards. Also, prices adjust in seconds. By the time a headline reaches your phone, professionals already traded it, likely against you, and you're paying yesterday's number. If your edge is being marginally slower than the world's fastest computers, that's not an edge.

The genuinely useful habit is inverted from what feeds teach: read less, from better sources, with more delay. A weekly review of one primary document teaches more than a daily scroll through reaction pieces.

## Do this today, fifteen minutes

Pick a company you use constantly. Go to its investor-relations page, ignore all the marketing, find the latest quarterly results PDF, and read just two sections, the segment revenue table and the forward-looking statements paragraph. Skip the CEO's enthusiastic prose entirely.

Then find a mainstream headline about that same earnings report and identify each adjective doing emotional work rather than adding data. Notice the article's framing had almost nothing to do with the tables you just looked at.

That's the gap, and once felt you stop needing advice about which headlines to trust.
