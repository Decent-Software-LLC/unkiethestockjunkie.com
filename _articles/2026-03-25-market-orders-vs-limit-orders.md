You set an alarm, open your broker at 9:31am, hit buy on a stock you'd researched all weekend, and pay twelve cents more than the screen showed. Nothing broke. You used a market order during the busiest minute of the day, in something that trades thinly, and nobody's going to send you a receipt explaining why.

## Two buttons, two different risks

**Market order**: "buy now, at whatever it costs." Guarantees execution, guarantees nothing about price. The quote you saw was one offer among many, and by the time your order reaches the exchange someone else may have taken it. Fine for index funds and huge companies. Genuinely bad otherwise.

**Limit order**: "buy only at this price or better." Sets your maximum, refuses anything worse. Risk flips entirely: you might not get filled at all, and the stock could climb without you while you watch. That's not malfunction, it's the tradeoff you chose.

A market order risks money on every fill. A limit order risks missing the trade. Once you hold that distinction, most beginner order mistakes disappear.

## Why the price moves between click and fill

Behind every quote sits an **order book**, a stack of resting buy and sell orders at various prices. If the best ask is 1,000 shares and your market order wants 5,000, you take those 1,000 then sweep upward through progressively worse sellers. Your average cost ends higher than the printed price. Same thing when selling, sweeping downward.

The gap between best bid and best ask is the **spread**. Apple might show a penny spread, effectively free. A small company could show a dollar plus on a $20 stock, meaning a 5% instant loss the moment you buy at the ask and could sell at the bid. There's no commission line telling you this; it's built into what you paid. [The Hidden Costs of Trading: Slippage, Spreads, and Market Impact](/articles/the-hidden-costs-of-trading-slippage-spreads-and-market-impact/) itemizes exactly this, since "commission-free" advertising doesn't mean free.

## Where beginners actually lose money

**Overnight gaps.** You place a market order at 4:05pm, after close. It executes at next morning's opening price, which can be far from Friday's close if earnings dropped or a CEO resigned overnight. People discover this the hard way once and never again. Limit orders with "day" vs "good-till-canceled" duration settings are the protection, and understanding sessions helps; see [Stock Market Hours: When Can You Trade?](/articles/stock-market-hours-when-can-you-trade/).

**Pre-market and after-hours.** Thin participation, enormous spreads, tiny orders moving prices violently. Quotes shown there often don't survive the open.

**News-minute market orders.** In the seconds after earnings or a Fed statement, liquidity thins just as volume explodes. A market order in that window fills at whatever the panic offers. Professionals widen or cancel their quotes at those moments; retail market orders walk right through.

**Stop-loss confusion.** A stop-loss becomes a *market* order once triggered, so a gappy open can sell you far below the level you set. A **stop-limit** specifies a floor instead but can fail to fill while the price runs away. The stop-loss mechanics get their own treatment in [Making Your First Trade](/guides/making-your-first-trade/) alongside a full walkthrough of placing that first trade, and the account setup itself is covered in [Opening Your First Brokerage Account](/guides/opening-your-first-brokerage-account/).

## The default that should annoy you

Most apps pre-select market orders because they always execute, and a customer who gets filled feels fine even when priced badly. Failure to fill is visible and frustrating. A bad fill isn't, usually invisible. Follow the incentive: choose limit orders as your habit, especially outside index funds and mega-caps, and especially in the first thirty minutes after the open when spreads are widest.

For stocks averaging millions of shares daily, the difference rarely matters. For thin names, always use limits, and check displayed size before sending anything large.

## Do this today, twenty minutes, zero dollars

Open a quote page for any thinly traded small company. Find **bid**, **ask**, and **volume**, then compute the spread as a percentage of price. Compare against Apple or an S&P 500 ETF doing the same. The ratio gap is usually dramatic, and it's a real cost you'd have paid silently.

Then paper-trade one purchase using a limit order set slightly below the ask. Watch whether it fills, partially fills, or vanishes. Then repeat with a market order and note what you actually paid versus what the screen had displayed. Feeling that difference with fake money is free tuition; discovering it later with real money isn't.
