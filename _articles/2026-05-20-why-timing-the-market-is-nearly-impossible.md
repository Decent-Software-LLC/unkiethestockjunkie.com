Someone who bought the S&P 500 every single day for twenty years, including the three days right before it collapsed, did worse than someone who bought on random dates chosen by coin flip. That isn't a joke and it isn't rare. It's roughly what happens to people who wait for a signal they're confident about.

## What market timing claims to be

Market timing means moving in and out of stocks based on predictions about direction: buy before the rally, sell before the crash. It sounds obviously smart because it describes what you'd do with concert tickets or airline fares. The difference is those have readable supply constraints behind them, while stock prices already contain whatever most people know plus guesses layered on top.

## Why it fails more often than it works

**You're guessing twice.** Exit needs to be right *and* re-entry needs to be right. Guessing each at 60% accuracy means getting both right about 36% of the time, and being wrong on the re-entry is the expensive half. Most people selling into a crash never come back; the panic that made them leave is still running when they'd have returned.

**The best days hide next to the worst days.** Widely cited studies of long-run US equity returns find that a large share of total gains arrives in a handful of sessions, disproportionately clustered immediately after brutal declines. Miss the ten best days over two decades and your return can land near half of simply staying put. You cannot identify those days in advance; in the moment they look like the crash continuing, which is exactly why fear-based exits keep deleting them.

**Your information isn't information yet.** By the time an outcome is obvious enough for you to act on it, prices moved weeks earlier. Earnings are known only after release, Fed decisions are priced within seconds, and a chart that "clearly" predicted a top will equally clearly have predicted something else if you redraw it after the fact. If you read it in a feed, thousands of algorithmic participants read it faster.

**Loss aversion guarantees you'll do it badly.** See [Loss Aversion: Why Losses Feel Worse Than Gains](/articles/loss-aversion-why-losses-feel-worse-than-gains/). Declines feel far worse than equivalent gains feel good, so you sell late, after pain becomes unbearable, and buy back late, after recovery feels safe. Both actions are individually rational in the moment and ruinous as a pattern.

## Three cases people get wrong in opposite directions

The **dot-com bubble** supports the skeptics. Investors in the late 1990s who concluded internet companies would compound forever were right about the internet and wrong about prices, and plenty never recovered. But the popular version of that story omits something important: much of the money lost was from buying *early in the mania*, not from failing to escape the top. People who timed it well by exiting in 1998 sat out the largest leg up and then missed the recovery too.

The **2008 financial crisis** looks easy to avoid in hindsight because the housing story was visible to anyone willing to connect subprime defaults to bank balance sheets. It was visible to very few people, and one of them, Michael Burry, profited while most funds holding the same thesis went broke waiting for it to play out. Being early and being right are different problems.

**Tesla** gets used both ways. It fell hard repeatedly for years and also produced some of the largest single-day percentage moves in large-cap history. Anyone who correctly exited one decline and stayed out learned nothing durable; anyone who correctly stayed through all of them made money regardless of skill. Volatility generates convincing stories in every direction. For the underlying numbers, see [Fundamental Analysis: Finding Intrinsic Value](/articles/fundamental-analysis-finding-intrinsic-value/).

## Run the experiment on yourself, honestly

Pick a date today. Write down, in an email to yourself: whether you believe the S&P 500 is higher or lower six months from now, roughly how much, and three reasons. Note exactly how you felt writing it.

Set a calendar reminder for six months out. Open the note before checking the price.

Most people discover two things. Their reasoning was mostly about news headlines that had already happened, and their confidence felt identical whether they turned out right or wrong. Confidence tracks how recently you read something persuasive, not how correct you were.

If you want to test actual trading decisions without money involved, do it properly in a simulator first; the mechanics and the psychology trap are covered in [Paper Trading: Practice Without Risk](/articles/paper-trading-practice-without-risk/). Just know paper trading measures discipline, not forecasting ability, since nothing about the fake dollars produces the fear that drives real mistakes.

Then read [Why Time in the Market Beats Timing the Market](/articles/why-time-in-the-market-beats-timing-the-market/) for the constructive side, which is about boring contributions on a schedule rather than about predicting anything.
