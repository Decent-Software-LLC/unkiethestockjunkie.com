A company's shares go public and everyone who wanted in can finally buy some. Which raises an obvious question, if you couldn't buy before, who could? And why did they agree to sell to strangers now? IPOs are less a doorway for outsiders than a liquidity event for people already holding the asset, and understanding that rearranges how you should think about the first day of trading.

## What actually happens

A private company has owners, usually founders, employees with options, and venture capital firms that put in money years earlier at much lower valuations. Going public means selling shares to institutions and then the public, registering documents with regulators, and joining an exchange where price is set continuously rather than negotiated privately every few months.

The proceeds of the primary offering flow to the **company** when it issues new shares. But a big chunk of most IPOs comes from existing holders selling their own stock, secondary sales whose proceeds go directly to those insiders rather than funding anything. Registration documents distinguish the two, which is worth knowing before assuming investment capital reached the business.

Early investors bought at low prices with the explicit plan of eventually exiting publicly at higher ones, accepting years of illiquidity and meaningful risk of total loss in exchange. The IPO is that bet resolving. When retail buyers pile into a first-day spike priced around maximum enthusiasm, they're frequently supplying the exit other parties were waiting for.

## Why pricing is genuinely hard

No market exists yet, so there's no observable price, only a guess produced by bankers paid on deal size, meaning incentives lean toward valuing high. Underwriters build a book of institutional demand, gauge what funds will pay, and set an initial range. Sometimes the first trade prints far above the offer price, indicating shares were left on the table; sometimes it falls below, and those quiet flops get much less coverage than the famous winners.

This uncertainty cuts against chasing debut names. Reported first-day pops make memorable headlines, while studies consistently show average returns after buying IPOs are poor over the following years once the lockup period expires and insiders face no further restrictions on selling. That pattern repeats across decades because the structure stays the same each time.

For context on how shares move after listing, see [Stock Market Hours: When Can You Trade?](/articles/stock-market-hours-when-can-you-trade/) since newly listed names behave nothing like established ones during early sessions.

## Terms you'll hit immediately

**Lock-up period.** Typically around ninety days after listing, during which insiders cannot sell. Expiration reliably increases available supply, and prices frequently weaken around it, which is disclosed but still surprises people reading only headlines.

**Float.** Only the portion actually trading freely matters for volatility, not all shares outstanding. Small float plus high interest produces violent swings in both directions regardless of business quality.

**Dual-class structure.** Founders frequently keep super-voting shares so public investors fund growth without gaining influence. Your economics participate fully while your votes barely register, and nothing on the quote screen flags this. It's disclosed in the prospectus, always. See [What Owning a Share Actually Gets You](/articles/from-lemonade-stand-to-shareholder-understanding-business-ownership/) for what ownership does and doesn't include.

## Do this today, fifteen minutes

Find a recent large IPO, search news from the last month or two. Locate its S-1 filing on EDGAR, the registration document companies must file before listing. Then answer three questions from the document itself rather than press coverage: how many shares are being sold by the company versus existing holders; what the stated use of proceeds is; and when the insider lock-up ends.

That last answer is a date you can write on a calendar, and knowing it puts you ahead of nearly everyone trading the name on vibes. Also read the **risk factors** section, the tedious part nobody reads, mostly boilerplate but occasionally revealing exactly what worries management enough to disclose it formally.

Most importantly, notice that after fifty minutes with the actual filing you'll understand the business better than someone who read ten headlines and a subreddit thread, and that gap compounds over hundreds of decisions. If filings feel impenetrable right now, start with [Income Statement: Revenue, Expenses, Profits](/articles/income-statement-revenue-expenses-profits/) so the numbers inside them mean something.
