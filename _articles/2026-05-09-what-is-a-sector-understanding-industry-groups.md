You've got 12 stocks. Sounds diversified. Then a single news story about drug pricing wipes 9% off your whole portfolio in an afternoon, because all 12 of them were pharmaceutical companies.

That's what sectors are for. Not how many things you own, but whether those things move together.

## Sector vs industry, in one breath

A **sector** is a big bucket: technology, healthcare, energy, financials. An **industry** is a smaller bucket inside it. The technology sector contains the software industry, the hardware industry, and the semiconductor industry.

So Apple, Microsoft and Nvidia share a sector but not an industry. Apple sells hardware, Microsoft sells mostly software and cloud, Nvidia sells chips. A chip shortage hurts Nvidia directly and Apple indirectly. That distinction is the difference between diversifying and just buying a lot of stuff.

There are 11 official sectors under the GICS system, which is the one almost every fund company and broker uses. You'll see them listed if you ever open a screener or a sector ETF page.

## Real buckets and who's in them

* **Technology**, Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia, with software, hardware and semiconductors as distinct industries underneath.
* **Healthcare**, Johnson & Johnson, Pfizer, UnitedHealth, spanning pharmaceuticals, biotech and medical devices. These three move on completely different news even though they share a sector label.
* **Consumer discretionary**, Amazon, McDonald's, Disney. This is the "spending when you feel fine" sector, retail, travel, leisure. It gets hit hardest in a recession because people cut fun first.
* **Consumer staples**, the boring counterweight: food, toothpaste, toilet paper. People buy it during a crash too.

Two words that look alike and behave nothing alike: *discretionary* and *staples*. Knowing which side a company is on tells you roughly how it will act when the market gets ugly.

## Why anyone bothers

Because sector is the hidden correlation. Two stocks can be totally unrelated businesses and still trade like twins if they share a sector and a macro trigger, interest rates crush regional banks and mortgage companies together regardless of their fundamentals.

Check this yourself in five minutes. Open any free charting tool and plot a semiconductor name against a bank over the last two years. Different shapes. Same-ish shape within each group. That clustering is the thing a sector map makes visible, and it's the reason [Diversification: Don't Put All Your Eggs in One Basket](/articles/diversification-don-t-put-all-your-eggs-in-one-basket/) talks about buckets rather than headcount.

## Do this today, for free

Go to a brokerage site, any of them, Webull, Fidelity, Schwab, and find the "Screener" or "Sector" tab. Look up the 11 sectors and see which has performed best over the past year. Note the number. Then set the range to five years and note that one too.

Those two lists are almost never the same. That's the entire lesson in one screen: sector leadership rotates, and whatever feels obvious right now is usually already expensive.
