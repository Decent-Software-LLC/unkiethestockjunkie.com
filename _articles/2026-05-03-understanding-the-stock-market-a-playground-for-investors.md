A market is where buyers and sellers get introduced. That's genuinely all an exchange does, match willing parties at prices neither fully controls. Add this up across millions of participants with different information, time horizons, tax situations and emotional states and you have why prices move constantly even when nothing news-worthy happens, which is worth internalizing before believing any headline claiming to explain today's direction.

## What "the market" means in your app

Usually a handful of indexes. **S&P 500** covers about 500 large US firms weighted by size, meaning megacaps dominate its movement regardless of how hundreds of other constituents perform. **Nasdaq Composite** skews technology; heavy semiconductor and software weighting explains most correlation between tech news cycles and your portfolio's color. The **Dow** averages just thirty companies price-weighted, an archaic construction whose biggest names influence it by share price rather than business importance despite endless coverage treating it as a referendum on the economy. See [Dow Jones: The Original Stock Index](/articles/dow-jones-the-original-stock-index/) for how that relic persisted.

If your holdings are broad low-cost funds, these numbers describe your outcomes decently. If concentrated, they're decoration, since half of individual stocks badly lag or beat their index while the average stock performs roughly in line with it. Averages hide enormous dispersion underneath.

## Why prices move instantly on nothing

Prices reflect what someone will transact at *now*, determined by marginal trades, tiny portions of total shares available. Large moves can start from modest volume if liquidity thins. Market makers quote both sides for compensation measured in spread width, adjusting quotes continuously against inventory risk and volatility models, pulling back during uncertainty, which amplifies moves precisely when you'd want tightest spreads. Add institutional rebalancing, options hedging flows forcing underlying purchases, index-fund mandated buying at close, retail flow chasing momentum, and high-frequency systems reacting to order-book microstructure within microseconds. Nearly every tick results from those interactions rather than anyone deciding the company changed worth.

Fundamentals anchor long-run outcomes, absolutely, but over days and weeks psychology plus mechanics dominate, and pretending otherwise guarantees confusion when good businesses drop for reasons unrelated to earnings. This is covered properly in [Bulls vs Bears: Understanding Market Trends](/articles/bulls-vs-bears-understanding-market-trends/).

## Two markets you actually touch

**Primary**, newly issued securities going public for the first time via IPO. Capital flows into the company and insiders sometimes cash out. Mechanics including lockup expirations appear in [What Is an IPO? When Companies Go Public](/articles/what-is-an-ipo-when-companies-go-public/).

**Secondary**, all subsequent trading among investors. When you buy shares no money reaches the business whatsoever, only another holder's pocket, which matters because it means daily activity does not fund operations except occasionally through dilutive issuance. Your ownership depends on custody chains running through your broker rather than anything happening at company headquarters.

Also know **bid**, **ask**, and **spread**, the auction behind every tap. Retail apps hiding that mechanism behind rounded interfaces don't remove its cost. See [The Hidden Costs of Trading: Slippage, Spreads, and Market Impact](/articles/the-hidden-costs-of-trading-slippage-spreads-and-market-impact/) if "commission-free" seemed free.

## The historical edge nobody teaches

Stocks have delivered positive average real returns across centuries including devastating wars, depressions, hyperinflations and pandemics, with short-term unpredictability coexisting comfortably with long-term upward drift. Not prophecy: historically, economies grew, prices rose somewhat, and businesses captured some gains. Nobody promised continuation under future conditions that might differ structurally from those producing past data, yet abandoning equity exposure after a downturn reliably destroys wealth more surely than enduring the dips. See [Famous Market Crashes: Lessons from History](/articles/famous-market-crashes-lessons-from-history/) and [Why Time in the Market Beats Timing the Market](/articles/why-time-in-the-market-beats-timing-the-market/), and note the honest asymmetry: recoveries took wildly varying lengths and some households were forced to sell during them permanently.

## Try this today, fifteen minutes

Pick three headlines from a finance site describing today's moves, then find the actual intraday percentage change on the S&P 500 from any free chart. Compare magnitude claimed to magnitude measured. Most days the real move is smaller than the vocabulary suggests. Headlines need drama; portfolios compound quietly without comment. Notice too that same-day commentary written hours after close cannot inform decisions you'll make tomorrow, when whatever justified it has already been traded around several times. If you'd like structure for what deserves attention instead, start with [Reading Financial News: Separating Signal from Noise](/articles/reading-financial-news-separating-signal-from-noise/).
