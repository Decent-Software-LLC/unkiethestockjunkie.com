Two stocks both trade at $5. One belongs to a company worth a few million dollars where next month's payroll is a genuine question. The other belongs to a company worth billions that chopped its share into tiny pieces because a friendlier-looking price seemed like good marketing.

The app shows "$5.00" for both. It shows nothing about which one is small, and small is where the danger hides. That's what market cap measures.

## The formula takes thirty seconds

**Market cap = shares outstanding × current share price**

1,000,000 shares at $10 each makes a $10,000,000 company. 4,000,000,000 shares at $10 makes a $40,000,000,000 company. Nobody should care that both display "$10.00," which is why comparing sticker prices between companies is meaningless.

A $500 share isn't inherently pricier than a $5 one in any way that matters. Confusing the two is why [Stock Splits: When Companies Multiply Your Shares](/articles/stock-splits-when-companies-multiply-your-shares/) exists: a company can turn one $1,000 share into ten $100 shares and its total value won't move a dollar.

## The size buckets

Sources disagree at the edges, but you'll see these bands roughly everywhere:

| Bucket | Market cap | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| Large-cap | $10B+ | Apple, Amazon, Google. Slow-moving, exhaustively covered, little edge for an outsider |
| Mid-cap | $2B–$10B | Survives a bad year, still small enough to double |
| Small-cap | $300M–$2B | Real volatility, wider spreads, single headlines move them hard |
| Micro-cap | under $300M | Illiquid, occasionally manipulable, high failure rate |

Where the risk differences are genuine rather than theoretical:

**Big companies** are boring partly because they're spread across products and countries, and partly because everyone who will ever own them already does. Prices swing less day to day, and payrolls usually keep clearing through a recession. What you surrender is the shot at 10x, which essentially never happens from a multi-trillion base, since multiplying something already gigantic by ten requires the whole economy to cooperate.

**Small companies** can genuinely grow into multiples, but rising interest rates hit them harder, bank relationships break faster, and often one product stands between them and irrelevance. Trading is thin, so exiting at a fair price can take longer than entering. The behavioral trap is that in an app they look identical to giants, same clean interface, same instant buy button, wildly different probability of going to zero. [Beta: Measuring Market Sensitivity](/articles/beta-measuring-market-sensitivity/) puts a number on exactly that swinginess.

For scale: Apple has spent time above $2 trillion in market cap, more than the annual output of most countries. GameStop and Castlight Health have occupied small-cap and micro-cap territory at various points, meaning positions in them behave less like ownership and more like event bets.

## Work one through

You hold 10,000 shares priced at $5. Market cap: 10,000 × $5 = **$50,000**. The entire company trades for less than a used car. Sit with what follows from that number: nobody's analysts cover it, no index fund is obliged to hold it, and one lost contract could reprice it overnight.

Same arithmetic at Apple's scale yields a figure bigger than most nations' GDP. The math is trivial; the implications are not. If you want to sanity-check whether any price is reasonable regardless of size, start with [Investing Myths Debunked](/articles/investing-myths-debunked/) before trusting a low number.

## Do this today, five minutes

Open any quote page, Yahoo Finance or your broker. Find **"Market Cap,"** then **"Shares Out,"** then price. Multiply the last two and confirm it equals the first. Then pull up a mega-cap name and a micro-cap name and compare their average daily volume columns.

Volume is the field people skip and shouldn't. If a stock trades a few thousand shares a day, your order is not getting filled at the quoted price, and the gap between quote and fill is a real cost you'll never see labeled.

No size band is correct to own. There's only a size band matching how much loss you can sit through without doing something you'd regret, and that's yours to decide.
