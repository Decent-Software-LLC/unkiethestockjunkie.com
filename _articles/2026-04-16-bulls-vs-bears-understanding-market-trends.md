Your portfolio drops 8% in three weeks. You check it on your phone between classes, feel genuinely sick, and start reading takes claiming it's going to zero. Meanwhile the same index has risen in most years since it existed.

Both things are true at once, and holding both is basically what understanding market trends means.

## Bull and bear, precisely

A **bull market** is a sustained advance with broad optimism. A **bear market** is conventionally a fall of **20% or more** from a recent peak. Between those sits the correction, usually defined as 10–20%, which happens far more often than either bookend and barely makes news.

That threshold matters because "it's falling" is not a bear market. Most declines stop well short, and people who treat every red month as a collapse end up selling at exactly the wrong time, permanently converting a paper loss into a real one.

As for the names, the usual account is direction of attack: a bull thrusting horns upward, a bear swiping paws downward. It's unproven, so don't trust anyone confidently telling you it comes from hibernation or livestock futures. Nobody knows.

## The part nobody tells beginners

Trend labels describe the *market*, an index like the S&P 500. They don't describe your stocks. Roughly half of individual holdings trade badly during a bull market, and plenty go to zero while everything else climbs. People say "the market went up last year" as though their own account participated. Frequently it didn't.

Which raises the trap: a bull market is where confidence gets built, and a bear market is where borrowed money gets destroyed. Anyone invested on margin, or using money needed next month for something real, learns that lesson in the worst available format. [FOMO in Investing: Fear of Missing Out](/articles/fomo-in-investing-fear-of-missing-out/) covers building confidence at the top instead, which is the expensive version.

## What moves the line

Indexes move on earnings, interest rates, and expectations about both. Rates matter more than beginners expect: when safe government bonds pay meaningfully again, money leaves stocks without any company doing anything wrong. Company results, oil prices, elections, a war, or one Fed meeting can each reprice the whole thing.

Amazon in the late 1990s is the clean bull example, a small online bookstore whose price climbed as it swallowed retail, then suffered violently when the dot-com bubble burst around 2000, then became one of the largest companies on earth anyway. Same business across three trend labels. The trend describes sentiment; it doesn't judge the company.

For the other side see [Famous Market Crashes: Lessons from History](/articles/famous-market-crashes-lessons-from-history/). Every US bear market including 1929, 2008 and March 2020 was followed by an index recovery, which is encouraging, and the honest footnote is that shareholders who sold in panic or couldn't afford to wait never saw their own recovery regardless.

## Run the arithmetic yourself

$1,000 into something rising 20% in a month becomes **$1,200**. $1,000 into something falling 15% becomes **$850**.

Now the asymmetry that trips people: after a 15% drop you need 17.6% to get back to even. After 20% you need 25%. After 50% you need 100%. Downside is not symmetric with upside, and that single fact explains why professionals obsess over not losing rather than chasing gains. [Loss Aversion: Why Losses Feel Worse Than Gains](/articles/loss-aversion-why-losses-feel-worse-than-gains/) explains why your brain handles this math badly under stress.

The harder question: if you invested $1,000 and it hit $850 tomorrow, what would you actually do? Be honest. That answer predicts your real behavior better than any strategy you've read about.

## Do this today, ten minutes

Pull up a free twenty-year chart of the S&P 500. Mark every stretch where it fell 20% or more from a peak, then look at what happened afterward and how long recovery took.

Two things should stand out. Those periods were obvious in hindsight and invisible in the moment, nobody was calmly buying while convinced the system was ending. And waiting for certainty cost more than enduring uncertainty did, which is argued properly in [Why Time in the Market Beats Timing the Market](/articles/why-time-in-the-market-beats-timing-the-market/).
