Every market crash in history was described, immediately afterward, as obvious. Read the actual newspaper headlines from a week before any of them and you'll find instead people explaining why this time conditions were fundamentally different. That gap is the entire lesson, and it's worth more than memorizing dates.

## 1929

The Roaring Twenties ran on margin, ordinary people borrowing most of a stock's purchase price. When prices turned, brokers issued margin calls, forcing sales, which triggered more calls, which forced more sales. October 28–29, 1929 wasn't the start of the decline so much as the moment leverage unwound all at once. The Dow did eventually recover its pre-crash level, but it took roughly 25 years, and the intervening Depression meant plenty of households had to sell during the worst stretch anyway.

The durable takeaway isn't "crashes happen," it's that **forced selling converts temporary paper losses into permanent real ones**, and borrowed money guarantees you'll be forced at the worst possible moment.

## 2000, dot-com

This one has a specific detail people get wrong. The Nasdaq Composite fell around 78% peak to trough between March 2000 and October 2002, and a majority of listed dot-com companies ended up worth nothing to shareholders. But "the internet was a scam" was the wrong reading: the technology worked, the adoption continued, and Amazon, which many wrote off as finished, became one of the largest companies on earth. Overvaluation and a bad idea are entirely different situations, and distinguishing them mid-collapse is close to impossible.

Anyone who concluded "never touch anything new" missed the biggest wealth creation of the following two decades. Anyone who concluded "any internet company is a buy" lost most of their money. Both mistakes came from the same evidence. See [FOMO in Investing: Fear of Missing Out](/articles/fomo-in-investing-fear-of-missing-out/) for how each version recruits believers.

## 2008

Housing loans got bundled into securities, priced by models assuming home prices wouldn't fall nationally, then sold across the global financial system with high leverage layered on top. When regional defaults climbed into those structures, the models had no answer, and institutions holding trillions in supposedly diversified assets discovered they all owned the same bet. Bear Stearns collapsed, Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail in September 2008, and credit markets froze within weeks because nobody could tell who was solvent.

Two lessons worth carrying. First, **correlations approach one in a crisis**: assets that behave independently while calm move together when everyone needs cash simultaneously, which quietly invalidates most diversification built during good times. Second, **"too big to fail" is a subsidy**, and if you ever wonder whether some banks enjoy cheaper funding than others, that's why. What the diversification argument survives is narrower than advertised but still real; see [Diversification: Don't Put All Your Eggs in One Basket](/articles/diversification-don-t-put-all-your-eggs-in-one-basket/).

## March 2020

The fastest large crash on record, roughly a month from peak to bottom, driven by a pandemic nobody's model included. It also produced the fastest recovery, with major indexes reclaiming prior highs within about five months. Investors who sold in March and stayed out due to fear sat through most of the rebound from the sidelines, while anyone who believed fundamentals were intact saw the collapse prove nearly irrelevant to long-run outcomes. Same event, opposite results, decided entirely by behavior rather than analysis.

Notice what these four have in common: none were caused by something predictable in advance. They came from leverage, from models with assumptions that stopped being true, from an event outside every forecast. You cannot avoid the next one by watching harder, because whatever arrives will look like nothing you've studied. For what actually helps, read [Asset Allocation: Balancing Stocks and Bonds](/articles/asset-allocation-balancing-stocks-and-bonds/) and [Bulls vs Bears: Understanding Market Trends](/articles/bulls-vs-bears-understanding-market-trends/).

## Do this today, twenty minutes

Search for "[crash name] 1929" or "2008 news coverage" and read three articles published *in* those moments, not retrospectives written later. Write down the confident claims. Notice they're confident in both directions: the crash deniers and the crash predictors both sounded certain.

Then look up where the S&P 500 sits now versus its 2007 peak, its 2000 peak, and its 1929 peak. Doing the arithmetic yourself teaches more than any summary: every one of those levels is far behind us, and each was recovered by people who kept buying while the headlines said otherwise. If that sounds easy, check what your own reaction would be; the honest version usually differs from the imagined one until you're actually down money.

*Educational content, not financial advice. Past recoveries don't guarantee future ones, and investing involves risk, including loss of the money you invest.*
