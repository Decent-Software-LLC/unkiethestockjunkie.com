A stock jumps 6% on no visible news. Half the comments say breakout, half say trap. The number that separates those two answers is sitting in a box under the price, and almost nobody looks at it before buying.

That box says how many shares actually changed hands. It's the difference between a move people agreed to and a move that happened because only three people were trading.

## What volume counts

Volume is simply the number of shares traded in a period, usually a day. Apple trading 55 million shares means 55 million changed owners; not 55 million buyers, since every share sold has someone on the other side. What it measures is conviction weighted by size, or more honestly, *activity*.

Two uses matter and everything else is decoration:

**Did anything real happen?** A 4% rally on triple normal volume means large participants transacted, which usually means information or rebalancing behind the move. The same 4% on half-normal volume is often noise from a thin afternoon, and thin moves reverse disproportionately. Same candle, completely different meaning.

**Can you get out?** This is the beginner-critical one. A company averaging a few hundred thousand shares a day trades thinly enough that your own order moves the price against you, and exiting at 3am during bad news can mean a fill far below whatever you saw. Index funds and mega-caps effectively never have this problem. See [The Hidden Costs of Trading: Slippage, Spreads, and Market Impact](/articles/the-hidden-costs-of-trading-slippage-spreads-and-market-impact/) for what that gap costs, since it's invisible on your trade confirmation.

## Where volume misleads

High volume does **not** mean "good." It appears in crashes. Volume is disagreement, and disagreement peaks right when something terrible becomes undeniable, so treating activity as approval is a reliable way to buy tops.

Low volume doesn't mean cheap opportunity either. Sometimes the reason nobody's trading a stock is that nobody wants it.

Chart pattern lore gets worse here. Volume "confirmations" in technical setups tend to look obvious afterward and ambiguous live, and if you draw support lines after seeing where price stopped they will always be exactly where you needed them. [Technical Analysis: Reading the Charts](/articles/technical-analysis-reading-the-charts/) covers the method seriously; just know that volume spikes carry far less predictive weight than most chart commentary implies.

## Two moments worth watching

**Earnings day.** Volume routinely runs several times normal. If a stock gaps up 10% on enormous volume and holds through the next session, that's participation. Gap up on modest volume and drift back down, that was a thin morning repricing.

**Index inclusion or removal.** When a fund adds a name to an index, mandated buyers must show up regardless of whether they like it. Volume explodes with zero new information about the business, and people constantly mistake that mechanical flow for enthusiasm about the company.

Also note timing: pre-market and after-hours volume is a fraction of regular-session volume, so prices printed there move easily on small orders and frequently don't survive the open. [Stock Market Hours: When Can You Trade?](/articles/stock-market-hours-when-can-you-trade/) explains why that matters when you set orders.

## Try this today, fifteen minutes

Open any free charting page, Yahoo Finance or TradingView both work without an account. Pull up Tesla and turn on the volume bars beneath the price. Find the tallest bar in the past year. Then search the date of that bar and read what actually happened.

You'll probably find earnings, a guidance cut, an analyst downgrade, or some headline you'd remember if you'd been paying attention. Now find the second-tallest bar and repeat.

Next time a stock you hold moves hard, glance at that bar column before doing anything. Asking "is anyone actually trading this?" takes two seconds and prevents an ungodly number of bad decisions.
