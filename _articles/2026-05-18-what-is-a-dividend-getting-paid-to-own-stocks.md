You buy $500 of Coca-Cola and then do absolutely nothing. Four times a year, cash shows up in the account. You didn't trade, didn't watch a chart, didn't get lucky on a meme coin. That's a dividend, and it's the only part of owning a stock that pays you before you sell.

## What's actually happening

A share is a partial ownership claim on a real business. When that business makes more cash than it needs to run, the board decides what to do with the rest: reinvest it into new products and stores, buy back shares, or hand it to owners. The handing-over part is the dividend. Most US companies pay quarterly, some annually or semiannually.

Nobody is obligated to keep paying. This matters more than it sounds. A dividend can be cut without warning, and usually gets cut exactly when the company is in trouble, which is precisely when you were counting on it.

## The two numbers you need

**Dividend yield**, annual dividend divided by the current share price, expressed as a percentage. Same math as a savings-account rate, completely different risk. A 4% yield means roughly $4 a year per $100 invested. If the price falls and the payout holds, the yield rises, which is why a suspiciously high yield is often a warning rather than a bargain. See [Investing Myths Debunked](/articles/investing-myths-debunked/) for the "free money" version of this mistake.

**Payout ratio**, the share of earnings sent out as dividends. A company earning $1.00 a share and paying $0.60 has a 60% payout ratio. Low ratio means room to grow or survive a bad year; a ratio above 100% means they're paying owners from borrowed money or reserves, which has a predictable ending.

## Real numbers

* **Coca-Cola (KO)** has paid dividends for over 100 years. At one point 100 shares threw off roughly $140 a year. Long histories like that belong to a small club, tracked in [Dividend Aristocrats: 25 Years of Rising Payments](/articles/dividend-aristocrats-25-years-of-rising-payments/).
* **Apple (AAPL)** pays quarterly, roughly $77 a year on 100 shares at the time of writing, despite being the more famous stock of the two. Apple spent decades paying nothing, then started in 2012 once it was generating far more cash than it could reinvest usefully.

Two of the most-owned stocks in the world, wildly different payout behavior, both perfectly reasonable choices. Dividend size isn't a quality score.

Worth knowing that many companies pay zero, permanently. Amazon paid no dividend during its entire growth phase, spending every dollar on warehouses instead, and did fine. Growth-stage companies paying large dividends is usually the odd signal, not the reverse.

## The quiet multiplier

If you take the cash, you get cash. If you reinvest it, you buy more shares, which pay more dividends, which buy more shares. Over ten or twenty years that loop accounts for a large share of what US stocks have ever returned to owners, because historically the payout was most of the total return while price appreciation filled in the rest.

Turning that on is usually a single toggle in your broker called DRIP. The mechanics and the tax wrinkle around it are in [DRIPs: Automatically Reinvesting Dividends](/articles/drips-automatically-reinvesting-dividends/).

There's also a trap here. Dividends paid in a taxable account are generally taxed the year received even if you never touched them, so "it's just reinvesting automatically" isn't quite free. In an account type designed for it, that problem goes away.

## Do this today, ten minutes

Open any quote page for KO, JNJ, or Verizon, three of the most common dividend names. Find three fields: **Div Yield**, **Payout Ratio**, and **Ex-Dividend Date**.

That last one is the detail that catches beginners. Ex-date is the cutoff: own the shares before it and you receive the next payment; buy on or after it and the previous owner does. People buy the day before a payout expecting instant cash and don't get it, mostly because nobody tells them about ex-dates until after.

Then check how many consecutive years the company has raised its payout. A short streak plus a fat yield usually means the market thinks the payment is at risk. That instinct, being suspicious of generosity, is the useful habit to leave with.
